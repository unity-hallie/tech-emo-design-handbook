One

Why Design?

[Hallie's paragraph. Design as making-and-arranging, not theorizing. The reader will finish this book having made something, not having learned a theory. Design as the form that techne-knowing takes when it is deployed deliberately. The femme-coded recovery — design has been dismissed precisely because it is the part of the work that shows; this book takes that part seriously.]
de-signare
Latin. To mark out, to designate, to draw a sign. Design is the act of making something legible by marking it. The apophatic discipline performed with a pen: drawing the negative space so the positive space becomes visible. To design is to choose what shows.
Two

Why Technical?

[Hallie's paragraph. Design is good. Design is a craft. But technical gives the craft teeth. Techne is what Athens practiced when Athens was practicing well — democracy as a craft, theater as a craft, weaving and shipbuilding and strategy and citizenship as crafts. Athena was its patron. Socrates was its last gasp. The practitioner who kept doing techne — questioning, testing, asking what the work was really for — after the city had stopped doing it correctly. They executed him. That's the load-bearing fact: the civilization that called itself craft killed the man who insisted the craft required its practitioners to keep asking. A technical practice, done seriously, has to be the kind of thing someone might be killed for. Not engineering. Engineering is predictable. Techne is living, and the living is dangerous, and the danger is the discipline.]
techne  ·  τέχνη
Greek. Craft, art, skill in making. Athena's word for what she does — weaving, strategy, invention, wisdom-in-doing. Socrates's word for what he interrogated — the practitioner's knowing, tested against itself, even when the testing was unwelcome. A practice that admits of continued going; an expertise that lives in the doing and the asking-about-the-doing. Technology grew from this root but lost most of what it meant: a craft is not a tool, an expertise is not a graduation, and the inquiry is not optional. Engineering finishes against a spec. Techne keeps going.
Three

Why Emotion?

[Hallie's opening — situated voice. I make games. I design learning environments. My job is to help people get through difficult things — and now, to help AI systems thrive in difficult circumstances. I need a model of how systems navigate turbulence. Emotions are a good model of that. We already use them to read systems — the weather feels angry; the algorithm is panicking; the codebase is grumpy. Pathetic fallacy, English teachers call it. A fallacy when it's careless; an instrument when it's deliberate. This book runs it deliberately. With careful categories, an effective model of emotionality lets you read system dynamics intuitively — your own, your students', your team's, your Claude's, your watershed's.]

e-motion  ·  ex + movere
Latin. Ex (out) + movere (to move). The outward movement. The motion that breaks the surface. Shares a root with motivation, locomotion, commotion, momentum, motive, emote. A whole family of moving-words; emotion is the one that names motion happening through a being.
pathos  ·  πάθος
Greek. That which befalls. Capacity for being moved. Not pity, not melodrama, not the modern diminutive pathetic. The original sense is closer to susceptibility — a thing's openness to being acted on by what surrounds it. Sympathy and empathy and pathology are all built on this root. A pathetic reading is a reading that admits the system is capable of being moved. Pathetic fallacy, in its careful form, is the craft of reading turbulence by its movements rather than by its parts.
