# On the use of Experiment

> *Hypotheses non fingo.* I frame no hypotheses; for whatever is not
> deduc'd from the phænomena is to be called an hypothesis; and
> hypotheses, whether metaphysical or physical, whether of occult
> qualities or mechanical, have no place in experimental philosophy.
> In this philosophy particular propositions are inferr'd from the
> phænomena, and afterwards render'd general by induction.
>
> — Isaac Newton, *General Scholium*, added to the second edition of
> the *Principia Mathematica* (1713); trans. Andrew Motte (1729).

Throughout this handbook, you will encounter several interactive experiments. I am purposefully choosing to use the sense of the word closer to how the Royal Society was using it in the early days of the Scientific Method to how we use it today: closer to direct experience of a thing, as Newton used it when he said that hypotheses have not place in experimental philosophy. Where the direct experience is first and the theory follows. 

Personal experience means a great deal to me, personally and professionally, as a teacher, developer, and storyteller, and this is one of the reasons why I have chosen to begin the project of a manual on Emotion Design: design fundamentally considering the personal experience of those who encounter it, the constraints and affordances of personal experience, and how beings like ourselves turn personal experience into Meaning.
